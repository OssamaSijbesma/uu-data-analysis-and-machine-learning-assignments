Data description¶

We have the following information provided by the teachers on the dataset. On top of that, we did ask about the origin of the dataset as it could provide us with extra insights, sadly that information was not provided.

Attributes for both student-mat.csv (Math course) and student-por.csv (Portuguese language course) datasets:

  1. school - student's school (binary: "GP" - Gabriel Pereira or "MS" - Mousinho da Silveira)
  2. sex - student's sex (binary: "F" - female or "M" - male)
  3. age - student's age (numeric: from 15 to 22)
  4. address - student's home address type (binary: "U" - urban or "R" - rural)
  5. famsize - family size (binary: "LE3" - less or equal to 3 or "GT3" - greater than 3)
  6. Pstatus - parent's cohabitation status (binary: "T" - living together or "A" - apart)
  7. Medu - mother's education (numeric: 0 - none, 1 - primary education (4th grade), 2 – 5th to 9th grade, 3 – secondary education or 4 – higher education)
  8. Fedu - father's education (numeric: 0 - none, 1 - primary education (4th grade), 2 – 5th to 9th grade, 3 – secondary education or 4 – higher education)
  9. Mjob - mother's job (nominal: "teacher", "health" care related, civil "services" (e.g. administrative or police), "at_home" or "other")
  10. Fjob - father's job (nominal: "teacher", "health" care related, civil "services" (e.g. administrative or police), "at_home" or "other")
  11. reason - reason to choose this school (nominal: close to "home", school "reputation", "course" preference or "other")
  12. guardian - student's guardian (nominal: "mother", "father" or "other")
  13. traveltime - home to school travel time (numeric: 1 - <15 min., 2 - 15 to 30 min., 3 - 30 min. to 1 hour, or 4 - >1 hour)
  14. studytime - weekly study time (numeric: 1 - <2 hours, 2 - 2 to 5 hours, 3 - 5 to 10 hours, or 4 - >10 hours)
  15. failures - number of past class failures (numeric: n if 1<=n<3, else 4)
  16. schoolsup - extra educational support (binary: yes or no)
  17. famsup - family educational support (binary: yes or no)
  18. paid - extra paid classes within the course subject (Math or Portuguese) (binary: yes or no)
  19. activities - extra-curricular activities (binary: yes or no)
  20. nursery - attended nursery school (binary: yes or no)
  21. higher - wants to take higher education (binary: yes or no)
  22. internet - Internet access at home (binary: yes or no)
  23. romantic - with a romantic relationship (binary: yes or no)
  24. famrel - quality of family relationships (numeric: from 1 - very bad to 5 - excellent)
  25. freetime - free time after school (numeric: from 1 - very low to 5 - very high)
  26. goout - going out with friends (numeric: from 1 - very low to 5 - very high)
  27. Dalc - workday alcohol consumption (numeric: from 1 - very low to 5 - very high)
  28. Walc - weekend alcohol consumption (numeric: from 1 - very low to 5 - very high)
  29. health - current health status (numeric: from 1 - very bad to 5 - very good)
  30. absences - number of school absences (numeric: from 0 to 93)

and this is the outcome / prediction target

  1. score - a normalized grade for an exam

The dataset consists of a mix of categorical and numerical features used to predict a standardized exam score. Our first impression is that the dataset has been heavily preprocessed, with most numerical variables standardized, except for age and absences.

The target variable combines scores from both the Portuguese and Mathematics courses. While the predictor variables do not appear inherently biased toward either subject, this assumption cannot be verified definitively since the dataset was provided in combined form. We have visualized the features that we expect has the largest influence on the score based on the correlation.

Model description¶

We are comparing 4 supervised learning models: K-nearest neighbours (KNN), random forest, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and Elastic Net regression.

KNN predicts the score of a student by averaging the K most similar students across all predictor variables and measure similarity based on a distance metric. KNN is a simple method that does not make any assumptions on the distribution of the data, however, it may not be the most suitable in our case. Our data has 30 features (dimensions), which exponentially increases the volume of the feature space so the density of the data points is likely very sparse. Furthermore, many of or features are categorical and KNN requires them to be one-hot encoded, which further increases the number of dimensions.

Random forest is a bagging ensemble that fits many decision trees on bootstrap samples of the training data and averages their predictions. At each split only a number of predictors is considered, which decorrelates the trees and reduces variance. This technique generally has a high accuracy rate and can handle a mix of categorical and numeric data well, which is an advantage compared to KNN. Given that our dataset mixes numerical and categorical variables, random forest is likely to be a good fit and requires little preprocessing beyond one-hot encoding for categorical variables.

XGBoost is a boosting ensemble that builds trees sequentially, where each new tree is fit to the residual errors of the previous ensemble. Compared to random forest, XGBoost reduces both bias and variance by combining many weak learners into a strong one. Boosting is considered to be state of the art when considering tabular data like ours, however, hyperparameters need to be tuned carefully so that the model does not overfit.

Elastic Net is a linear regression model with two penalties added to the squared error - an L1 penalty that shrinks weak coefficients to exactly zero and an L2 penalty that shrinks them smoothly. It suits our data because with 39 columns for only 316 students, the penalties reduce overfitting and automatically drop irrelevant features. The L2 part also handles correlated predictors more stably than Lasso alone. The penalty strength (alpha) and the mix between the two penalties (l1_ratio) are tuned with cross-validation inside each training fold.

# Define the objective function for Optuna optimization (Bayesian Optimization worked better than recursive feature elimination with cross-validation RFECV)
def objective(trial):
    # Hyperparameter search space
    params = {
        "max_depth": trial.suggest_int("max_depth", 1, 10),
        "min_child_weight": trial.suggest_int("min_child_weight", 1, 20),
        "learning_rate": trial.suggest_float("learning_rate", 0.005, 0.2, log=True),
        "subsample": trial.suggest_float("subsample", 0.4, 1.0),
        "colsample_bytree": trial.suggest_float("colsample_bytree", 0.2, 1.0),
        "colsample_bylevel": trial.suggest_float("colsample_bylevel", 0.5, 1.0),
        "gamma": trial.suggest_float("gamma", 1e-4, 10.0, log=True),
        "reg_alpha": trial.suggest_float("reg_alpha", 1e-3, 50.0, log=True),
        "reg_lambda": trial.suggest_float("reg_lambda", 1e-2, 100.0, log=True),
        "max_bin": trial.suggest_int("max_bin", 64, 256),
        "n_estimators": trial.suggest_int("n_estimators", 100, 1000, step=50),
    }

    kf = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=RANDOM_SEED)
    scores = []

    for train_idx, val_idx in kf.split(X_XGB, y):
        # Split the data into training and validation sets for the current fold
        X_train, X_val = X_XGB.iloc[train_idx], X_XGB.iloc[val_idx]
        y_train, y_val = y.iloc[train_idx], y.iloc[val_idx]

        # Fit the model on the training set and validate on the validation set
        xgb_model = xgb.XGBRegressor(
            tree_method="hist",
            enable_categorical=True,
            n_jobs=2,
            eval_metric="rmse",
            random_state=RANDOM_SEED,
            **params,
        )

        # Train the model on the training set and validate on the validation set
        xgb_model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_val, y_val)], verbose=False)

        # Make predictions on the validation set
        preds = xgb_model.predict(X_val)

        # Calculate RMSE for the current fold
        rmse = root_mean_squared_error(y_val, preds)

        # Append the RMSE of the current fold to the scores list
        scores.append(rmse)

    return np.mean(scores)


# Terminate unpromising hyperparameter trials mid-evaluation
pruner = optuna.pruners.MedianPruner(
    n_startup_trials=5,  # Wait 5 trials before pruning starts
    n_warmup_steps=10,  # Let trees build for at least 10 rounds per trial
)

# Create Optuna study with median pruner
study = optuna.create_study(
    direction="minimize",
    sampler=optuna.samplers.TPESampler(seed=RANDOM_SEED),
    pruner=pruner,
)

# Optimize the objective function using Optuna (1000 trials)
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=1000, n_jobs=-1, show_progress_bar=True)

# Output Results
print(f"Optimized CV RMSE: {study.best_value:.4f}\n")

print("Hyperparameter selection:")
for key, value in study.best_params.items():
    print(f"{key} = {value}")

# Build pipeline where RFE selects important features, then the model is fit on them
# The RF inside RFE only needs to give a reasonable importance ranking,
# so fewer trees and limited depth is used to decrease runtime
rf_pipeline = Pipeline(
    steps=[
        (
            "feature_selection",
            RFE(
                estimator=RandomForestRegressor(
                    n_estimators=50, max_depth=10, random_state=RANDOM_SEED
                ),
                step=3,
            ),
        ),  # removing 3 features per round decreases run time
        ("model", RandomForestRegressor(random_state=42)),
    ]
)

# Provide a range of parameters for hyperparameter tuning
param_distributions = {
    # Choose randomly the number of features to select with RFE
    "feature_selection__n_features_to_select": [5, 10, 15, 20, 25],
    "model__n_estimators": [100, 200, 300, 500],
    "model__max_features": ["sqrt", "log2", 0.5, 1.0],
    "model__max_depth": [None, 5, 10, 15],
    "model__min_samples_split": [2, 5, 10],
    "model__min_samples_leaf": [1, 2, 4],
}

# Within one fold of the CV, RFE runs iteratively and picks out n features,
# and the RF is trained on those features.
# Running the RFE one fold at a time prevents data leakage during CV.
rf_search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    estimator=rf_pipeline,
    param_distributions=param_distributions,
    n_iter=100,  # Only 100 iterations is used since RFE increases run time
    cv=5,
    scoring="neg_mean_squared_error",
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=42,
    verbose=1,
)

rf_search.fit(X_dummy, y)

best = rf_search.best_index_
rf_tuned_mse = -rf_search.best_score_
rf_tuned_rmse = np.sqrt(-rf_search.best_score_)
rf_tuned_std = rf_search.cv_results_["std_test_score"][best]

print(f"Best Parameters: {rf_search.best_params_}")
print(f"Best CV MSE: {rf_tuned_mse:.4f}")
print(f"Best CV RMSE: {rf_tuned_rmse:.4f}")
print(f"STD across folds: {rf_tuned_std:.4f}")

# Selected features from the best pipeline (refit on all the data)
best_pipeline = rf_search.best_estimator_
rfe_selector = best_pipeline.named_steps["feature_selection"]
selected_feature_names = X_dummy.columns[rfe_selector.support_]

print(f"\nRFE selected {len(selected_feature_names)} features:")
print(list(selected_feature_names))

    StandardScaler(),
    ElasticNetCV(
        l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1],
        alphas=np.logspace(-4, 1, 100),
        cv=5,
        max_iter=20000,
        random_state=42,
    ),
)

# Cross-validated MSE (10-fold, repeated 5 times)
cv = RepeatedKFold(n_splits=10, n_repeats=5, random_state=42)

    enet, X_en, y_en, cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error", n_jobs=-1
)
